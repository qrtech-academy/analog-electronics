\chapter{Building an operational amplifier, and the capstone}
\label{c10:ch:amplifier}

{\large\itshape The black box of Chapter~3, opened and rebuilt from the six chapters since.\par}

\begin{chapterbox}{In this chapter}
\begin{itemize}
  \item The black box of Chapter~\ref{c3:ch:filters}, opened: eleven transistors, and every one of
    them already analysed.
  \item What each stage is responsible for, and why that is the only way to size any of them.
  \item The gain budget, and the eleven decibels that loading takes.
  \item The two stages with no voltage gain, which are worth 31 dB each.
  \item Why the amplifier has no open-loop operating point at all, and what that means for the
    solver.
  \item Miller compensation: making one pole dominant on purpose.
  \item Closing the loop, and Chapter~\ref{c4:ch:feedback}'s arithmetic arriving where it was
    always going.
  \item The capstone: one program, every stage predicted and solved, printed side by side.
\end{itemize}
\end{chapterbox}

\textbf{Everything in this chapter is already built.} The pair is Chapter~\ref{c9:ch:diffpair}, the
mirror is Chapter~\ref{c7:ch:smallsignal}, the followers are Chapter~\ref{c8:ch:follower}, and the
gain stage is Chapter~\ref{c7:ch:smallsignal} again. This chapter adds no new circuit; it adds the
\textbf{accounting}, and the accounting is where the surprises are.

There are three of them. The first is the \textbf{budget}: two stages of 65.7 dB should give 131,
and the amplifier delivers 120, with 10.6 of the missing 11.5 in one place --- the gain stage
driving the output stage, and that is \emph{after} the output stage was made a Darlington. This is
Chapter~\ref{c1:ch:circuits}'s loading for the sixth and last time. The second is that \textbf{the
two stages with no voltage gain are the most valuable in the amplifier}: they are six of the eleven
transistors, they amplify nothing, and each is worth about 31 dB. Gain is easy; connecting two
stages together is what is hard.

The third is that \textbf{the amplifier has no open-loop operating point}. A gain of a million and
rails of $\pm 15$ V means that 15 microvolts of input offset puts the output against a rail, and no
pair of transistors matches to 15 microvolts. A solver that reports the output at $-15$ V has not
failed, and a stage that cannot be biased on its own is not a stage --- it is part of a loop.

Then close the loop, and Chapter~\ref{c4:ch:feedback}'s arithmetic arrives where it was always
going: 120 dB open, a gain of 20 closed, an error of 0.002 per cent, and a closed-loop gain that
moves by nine parts in a hundred thousand while the open-loop gain moves by a factor of fourteen.
The capstone in \secref{c10:sec:exercises} is one program that predicts the whole amplifier, solves
the whole amplifier, and reconciles the two row by row. \textbf{The differences are the book}, and
a reader who can account for every row has understood the ten chapters.
