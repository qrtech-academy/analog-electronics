\chapter{Followers, and the output stage they turn into}
\label{c8:ch:follower}

{\large\itshape The stage with no voltage gain, and why almost every design has one.\par}

\begin{chapterbox}{In this chapter}
\begin{itemize}
  \item The stage with no voltage gain, and why nearly every amplifier contains several.
  \item Gain just under one, and the shortfall as a design quantity rather than a rounding error.
  \item Impedance transformation: $h_{FE}$ going in, $r_e$ coming out.
  \item The 8 ohm problem, and the arithmetic that says one follower is not enough.
  \item The Darlington, $h_{FE}^2$, and the price of leaning on beta.
  \item Class A, class B, and the dead band between them.
  \item Class AB, and the 26 millivolt rule that sizes the emitter resistors.
  \item Why the bias diodes must be bolted to the same heatsink as the output devices.
  \item The source follower, the body effect, and the one place $r_s$ does not carry across
    cleanly.
  \item Building \code{ael::follower} and \code{ael::output}.
\end{itemize}
\end{chapterbox}

This chapter is Chapter~\ref{c7:ch:smallsignal}'s model applied at the emitter instead of the
collector, and almost nothing in it is derived that was not derived there. A \textbf{follower} has
a voltage gain of 0.97 and is the most-used circuit in the book, because everything it is for is in
its two resistances rather than in its one gain: it shows the stage driving it $h_{FE}$ times what
is on its emitter, and shows its load a source resistance $h_{FE}$ times smaller.

The reason that matters is one calculation. Take the stage of Chapter~\ref{c7:ch:smallsignal},
with a gain of 38.5 and an output resistance of 9.89 kilohm, and connect a loudspeaker: it delivers
\textbf{0.08 per cent} of its gain and the amplifier does not work at all. One follower recovers
4 per cent, which is fifty times better and still useless. A \textbf{Darlington} recovers 68.
\textbf{That is the whole reason output stages exist}, and it is Chapter~\ref{c1:ch:circuits}'s
loading arithmetic for the fifth time in this book.

The second half sizes a real output stage. Two followers sharing the work have a dead band 1.3 V
wide whose \emph{position}, not its width, is the problem; closing it costs a small idle current,
and the rule that sets it makes the emitter factor exactly 2. Then two things that decide whether
the stage survives: a thermal loop that can run away, whose fix is not a circuit change at all but
where a component is bolted, and \textbf{the place where the constant-drop model finally
breaks} --- not by a few per cent, as it has until now, but by a factor of sixty, for a reason that
is about the calculation rather than about the model.

everything here, and no exercise is set on it.
