\chapter{The differential amplifier}
\label{c9:ch:diffpair}

{\large\itshape The input stage of every operational amplifier, and the first circuit that rejects
rather than amplifies.\par}

\begin{chapterbox}{In this chapter}
\begin{itemize}
  \item The first circuit in the book built to \textbf{reject} rather than to amplify.
  \item The pair, the tail, and the two halves that share it.
  \item Differential gain: the common-emitter result with a two in it, and where the two comes
    from.
  \item Common-mode gain, and the tail acting as a degeneration resistor of twice its value.
  \item CMRR, and the fact that the collector resistor cancels out of it entirely.
  \item Why every real pair has a current source in its tail and not a resistor.
  \item The current-mirror load, and the two independent factors it wins.
  \item The large-signal transfer: nine millivolts of linearity, and hard limiting beyond.
  \item Offset, matching, and why almost every modern input stage is MOSFET.
  \item Building \code{ael::diffpair}.
\end{itemize}
\end{chapterbox}

A stage with two inputs and one job: amplify the difference, ignore what the two have in common.
Everything else follows from that sentence, and from one component neither transistor is connected
to at the top --- the \textbf{tail}. The tail fixes the total current; the inputs decide only how it
is divided. When the two inputs move oppositely the tail node does not move at all, and when they
move together it has to move with them. \textbf{That asymmetry is the circuit}, and both gains fall
straight out of Chapter~\ref{c7:ch:smallsignal}'s model with nothing new derived.

Rejection then has a surprise in it. Write the ratio of the two gains and \textbf{the collector
resistor cancels, exactly and completely}: no choice of load improves rejection, and neither does
the gain. The only lever is the tail --- and because a resistance in the tail implies a voltage
across it, 80 dB of rejection from a resistor needs a 520 V supply. That is why the tail is a
current source, and it generalises into the most useful sentence in Part~2: \textbf{a current
source has a large resistance without a large voltage across it.}

The same device then appears as a load, where it wins two independent factors that textbooks
routinely run together, and the chapter separates them. Two things the small-signal model cannot
see close it: the pair is linear over about nine millivolts and hard-limits beyond, which is where
an amplifier's slew rate comes from; and the offset that mismatch produces, whose largest term is
not a mismatch between the transistors at all.
