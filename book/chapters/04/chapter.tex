\chapter{Feedback, active filters, and the diode}
\label{c4:ch:feedback}

{\large\itshape Why the ideal rules work, what they cost, and the first nonlinear device.\par}

\begin{chapterbox}{In this chapter}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Loop gain, and the closed-loop gain written so the error term is visible.
  \item Desensitisation: trading gain you have for accuracy you want.
  \item What feedback does to input and output impedance, and in which direction.
  \item Distortion divided by the loop gain, and the nonlinearity that always survives.
  \item Gain-bandwidth product, and the stability limit that ends the free lunch.
  \item Active filters, and the loading problem an amplifier solves for them.
  \item The diode: the exponential, the 0.7 V approximation, and where it stops being one.
  \item Newton-Raphson, and why plain Newton-Raphson does not work on a diode.
  \item Building \code{ael::feedback}, \code{ael::device::diode} and \code{ael::nr}.
\end{itemize}
\end{chapterbox}

Chapter~\ref{c3:ch:filters} asserted two rules and used them. This chapter derives them, and the
derivation hands back an error term that says exactly how far from true they are.
\textbf{One number decides everything}: the loop gain $T$. The gain error is $1/(1+T)$, distortion
is divided by $1+T$, impedances are multiplied or divided by $1+T$. Learn $T$ and the rest is
bookkeeping --- and learn what it costs, which is the gain you gave up and the bandwidth you did
not get, because gain and bandwidth trade one for one and an amplifier is sold by their product.

Feedback also stops working, and it is worth knowing where. Enough phase shift around the loop
turns negative feedback positive and an amplifier into an oscillator; one pole can never get
there, two only asymptotically, and three get there with gain to spare. That is why
Chapter~\ref{c10:ch:amplifier}'s four-stage amplifier has a capacitor in it whose only job is to
make the amplifier slow.

The second half of the chapter introduces \textbf{the first device in this book that is not
linear}. A diode has no phasor description, because it creates frequencies that were not in the
excitation, and it cannot be solved in one pass, because its conductance depends on the answer. The
machinery for that is Newton-Raphson: guess, linearise, solve with the linear solver you already
have, repeat. \textbf{Every nonlinear simulator is that loop}, and the one detail that makes it
work in practice --- damping a large step by taking it in the logarithm --- is the difference
between seven iterations and a hundred and sixty-eight.
