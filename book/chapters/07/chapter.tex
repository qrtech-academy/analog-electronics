\chapter{Small-signal analysis: \texorpdfstring{$r_e$}{r\_e}, the emitter factor, and the cascode}
\label{c7:ch:smallsignal}

{\large\itshape The centre of the book, and the model the whole of Part~2 is built on.\par}

\begin{chapterbox}{In this chapter}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Small-signal as a straight line through an operating point, and everything that discards.
  \item Building the small-signal schematic: what to short, what to delete, what to replace.
  \item The intrinsic emitter resistance, and where the 26 mV comes from.
  \item Gain, input resistance and output resistance, each from one walk around a loop.
  \item The emitter factor: one number for what degeneration costs and what it buys.
  \item Which resistance that factor actually multiplies, which is not the obvious one.
  \item The Early effect, and the current-mirror load that stops the boost being thrown away.
  \item The source factor and $r_s$: the same results, transferred to the MOSFET by substitution.
  \item The Miller effect, and the cascode as the answer to it.
  \item Building \code{ael::ssm}, checked against the solver on every stage.
\end{itemize}
\end{chapterbox}

\textbf{Everything before this chapter was preparation; everything after it is this applied.} If
one chapter is worth rereading, it is this one.

A transistor is exponential. Over a small enough excursion an exponential is a straight line, and
the slope of that line at the operating point is all a signal ever sees. That slope has units of
conductance, and its reciprocal is $r_e$, 26 ohm at 1 mA. \textbf{Every gain, every input
resistance and every output resistance in the rest of the book is written in terms of it}, and each
of the three comes from one walk around one loop, done the same way every time.

The organising idea is the \textbf{emitter factor}, $EF = (r_e + R_E)/r_e$: one number that
answers two questions at once, by what factor the gain falls and by what factor a resistance rises.
The first half is exactly right. \textbf{The second half is attached to the wrong node in most
treatments}, and the difference is a factor of ten. Getting it right is worth doing carefully,
because it is what explains the current-mirror loads that would otherwise appear from nowhere in
Chapters~\ref{c9:ch:diffpair} and \ref{c10:ch:amplifier}.

Two more results close the chapter, and neither needs new machinery. The \textbf{MOSFET} arrives by
naming $r_s = 1/g_m$, after which every result above transfers by substitution, with input
resistance the one exception. And the \textbf{Miller effect} turns four picofarads into 1.5
nanofarads and rolls a stage off at a hundred kilohertz, for which the answer is a
\textbf{cascode} --- which turns out to be a degenerated stage whose degeneration resistor is
another transistor's $r_o$.
