\chapter{Biasing, and what an emitter resistor actually buys}
\label{c6:ch:bias}

{\large\itshape The quiescent point, and the first place the obvious argument needs correcting.\par}

\begin{chapterbox}{In this chapter}
\begin{itemize}
  \item The quiescent point as three numbers, and the load line that constrains them.
  \item Voltage-divider bias, and the base current that loads the divider you designed.
  \item Stiffness: why the divider current must be much larger than the base current, and what
    happens at the usual rule of thumb.
  \item Thermal drift: the base-emitter voltage falling about 2 mV per degree, and what that does.
  \item The emitter resistor as local feedback, and the drift divided by the emitter factor.
  \item An argument that does not survive its own arithmetic, and the correct one.
  \item The 220 mV rule, and the E12 value it lands on.
  \item Building \code{ael::bias}, and adding temperature to the transistor model.
\end{itemize}
\end{chapterbox}

An amplifier is a transistor sitting at an operating point with a signal moving it about. This
chapter is the sitting still; Chapter~\ref{c7:ch:smallsignal} is the moving about. The
construction is the load line of Chapter~\ref{c5:ch:transistor} used at a different place on it: a
switch used the two ends, and an amplifier sits where there is room to move both ways.

Divider bias has a surprise in it immediately. The divider that gives 1.71 V unloaded gives 1.60 V
with a base hanging on it, and the collector current comes out 12 per cent below what the design
said --- \textbf{Chapter~\ref{c1:ch:circuits}'s loading, for the fourth time}, and this time the
arithmetic is self-referential, because the base current depends on the collector current which
depends on the base voltage which depends on the base current.

Then the problem worth solving. \textbf{At a fixed base voltage the collector current rises about
8 per cent per degree}, which over a thirty-degree rise is a factor of ten: a stage biased at 1 mA
on a bench at 20 degrees is at 10 mA inside an enclosure at 50, sitting hard against one end of its
load line. The fix is an emitter resistor, and \textbf{there is a tempting explanation of what it
does that does not survive its own numbers}. \Secref{c6:sec:b2} sets that argument out in full and
then breaks it with one exponential; \secref{c6:sec:b3} gives the version that survives, which is
Chapter~\ref{c4:ch:feedback}'s: the resistor is a local feedback loop, and it divides the drift by
exactly the factor it costs in gain.
